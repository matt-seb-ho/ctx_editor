Thank you for the extensive and substantive rebuttal. The expanded LiC evaluation, paired comparisons, and new random end-to-end experiment materially strengthen the submission. I now regard my original concern about small LiC samples and exclusive reliance on replay as largely addressed, although some statistical details remain necessary.

My strongest remaining concern is the lack of a stronger context-management or matched-compute baseline. The distinction the authors draw between context-length compaction and context pollution is conceptually reasonable, and I agree that not every compaction method is necessarily a direct competitor. However, this distinction alone is not sufficient to rule out an empirical comparison. Methods such as U-Fold perform intent summarization while selectively retaining relevant tool state, and therefore overlap sufficiently with AC3 to be informative, especially in the tau2 setting. Likewise, the promised matched-compute condensation baseline and high-pollution self-reflection experiment are not yet reported. The structural-exclusion ablation is valuable evidence that additional calls in a contaminated information-flow configuration can hurt, but it does not by itself establish that AC3’s gains cannot be achieved by a strong equal-budget reflection, summarization, or state-preserving folding baseline.

The expanded LiC evaluation and paired sign test substantially address my earlier concern about statistical reliability by showing consistent gains across model–task–prefix settings. I would nevertheless appreciate clarification of the unit of analysis: the sign test appears to operate over 36 aggregate conditions that may share underlying problems across models and prefixes, so it primarily demonstrates directional consistency rather than instance-level uncertainty. Please also clarify why Gated-Reset has only 12 comparisons, and report the number of examples and paired outcomes in the random end-to-end subset.

For WildChat, reporting mean ± standard deviation over three seeds is helpful, although conversation-level uncertainty against both AO and full context would further strengthen the result. For tau2, the corrected per-model reporting is a meaningful improvement over the original best-of-three presentation. The main remaining concern is that the rebuttal reports the best AC3 operator separately for each respondent model. Please clarify how the operator would be selected without test-set knowledge and, where available, provide the other operator results or paired task-level uncertainty. Please also explain how the rate-limit-clipped Kimi baseline was handled when calculating the claimed improvement. I do not view every possible additional statistical analysis as necessary during rebuttal, but these clarifications would determine how strongly the new headline claims can be interpreted.

The component table also makes the intended AC3 framework substantially clearer. I understand the authors’ position that the same two-stage analytical structure is reused while the intervention operator is a design knob. Nevertheless, I view the “single method” concern as only partially resolved. Structural exclusion is enabled or disabled according to the conversational regime, tau2 adds explicit environment-state tracking and capped resets, and the tau2 headline results select different operators for different models. These adaptations may all be reasonable, but “one code path” is not by itself evidence of one fixed, deployable algorithm. It would help to specify how AC3 determines, from observable conversation features, whether structural exclusion is appropriate and which intervention intensity to use. Without such a rule, I think the contribution is more accurately characterized as a modular family of context-management strategies rather than one configuration that operates unchanged across all regimes. Correspondingly, the revised headline should distinguish between “one AC3 configuration improves everywhere” and “at least one AC3 operator improves in every evaluated regime.”

Overall, the rebuttal represents substantial progress. It corrects the inappropriate best-of-three presentation, provides much stronger LiC evidence, and makes the framework easier to understand. The paper is therefore considerably stronger than it was at initial review. At present, however, the absence of an actual stronger or matched-compute baseline and the remaining ambiguity around deploy-time configuration prevent me from considering my principal concerns fully resolved. I am therefore inclined to retain my current borderline-reject rating.
